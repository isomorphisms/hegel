# Mara Beller: *At Whom Are We Laughing?*

Mara Beller's 1998 *Physics Today* article, "The Sokal Hoax: At Whom Are We Laughing?", is one of the better responses to the Sokal affair because it refuses the easiest division of the participants into scientists who know what words mean and postmodernists who do not.

Her argument is not that Alan Sokal's hoax failed, nor that scientific nonsense becomes respectable when written by a cultural theorist. Her argument is that the history of twentieth-century physics makes the joke much less clean than its admirers wanted it to be.

## The central move

Sokal's parody worked partly by moving from genuine statements about quantum mechanics into increasingly extravagant claims about politics, psychology, culture and reality. The transition was funny because the later claims sounded like absurd extrapolations from physics.

Beller asks us to look at who had already been making exactly this kind of extrapolation.

Her examples are not marginal figures. They include Max Born, Niels Bohr, Werner Heisenberg and Wolfgang Pauli: founders of quantum mechanics whose scientific authority was enormous.

Born tried to extend complementarity into political theory. Pauli wrote seriously about connections among physics, religion, Jungian psychology and symbolic reality. Bohr treated complementarity not merely as a feature of a particular physical formalism but as a very general epistemological principle with analogues in psychology, anthropology, art and other domains. Heisenberg likewise suggested that the conceptual revolution of quantum mechanics should transform wider culture.

So when a later writer takes quantum mechanics as authorization for sweeping claims about politics or subjectivity, Beller's point is that the writer is not inventing the genre from nothing. Distinguished physicists had already licensed it.

## Asymmetrical standards for obscurity

One of Beller's sharpest points concerns authority.

Obscure prose by Derrida or a cultural theorist is treated by many physicists as evidence of emptiness. Obscure prose by Bohr is often treated as evidence that the reader has not yet understood something deep enough.

Beller gives a striking anecdote. A famous reprint of Bohr's reply to the Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen paper appeared with pages out of order, yet the error was rarely noticed despite the text's canonical status and frequent citation. Her point is not that Bohr therefore had nothing to say. It is that prestige changes the reader's default explanation of incomprehension. When an authorized figure is difficult, the reader blames himself. When a disfavored figure is difficult, the reader blames the author.

This is exactly the kind of social fact about knowledge that the science-studies people were interested in.

## Beller is not defending scientific illiteracy

The article is sometimes easier to caricature than to read. Beller does not say that any analogy between quantum mechanics and culture is legitimate. She explicitly describes many later extrapolations as scientifically illiterate, excessive or nonsensical.

Her dilemma is narrower and stronger:

1. If it is illegitimate to infer broad political, psychological or cultural lessons from quantum mechanics, then some of the most revered physicists of the twentieth century are guilty of the same kind of overreach.
2. If such cross-domain reasoning can sometimes be meaningful, then it cannot be dismissed merely because the person attempting it works in cultural studies rather than physics. It has to be judged argument by argument.

The disciplinary identity of the speaker cannot do the intellectual work for us.

## Reality, Einstein, and the Copenhagen orthodoxy

Beller also complicates the standard story about realism.

Sokal and Steven Weinberg defended an observer-independent reality against what they regarded as fashionable relativism. Beller points out that Einstein, despite being a committed realist, did not hold a crude picture in which a scientific theory is simply compared against reality as though both were independently laid out before us. He understood theoretical description as conceptually mediated and argued for objectivity through invariant structure rather than through a naive mirror theory of knowledge.

At the same time, Bohr and other defenders of the Copenhagen interpretation often presented much more radical philosophical conclusions as though they followed inevitably from the quantum formalism: the impossibility of a unified observer-independent picture, the necessity of complementarity, the overthrow of ordinary causality, and so on.

Beller objects especially to this **rhetoric of inevitability**. An interpretation of a physical theory was often presented as if it were simply the logical content of the physics itself.

The existence of Bohmian mechanics matters to her argument because it demonstrates that the formal success of quantum theory does not force one unique Copenhagen philosophy. Whatever one thinks of Bohmian mechanics, its existence is enough to show that philosophical conclusions cannot simply be smuggled in under the authority of the equations.

## What Sokal exposed, and what he did not

Sokal exposed a real editorial and intellectual failure. A journal published a parody containing statements its author considered nonsensical, and the political and rhetorical fit of the article plainly helped it through.

Beller's point is that this does not establish a neat geography in which nonsense lives on one side of a disciplinary border and rigor on the other.

Physicists themselves had produced grand metaphysical, psychological and political claims backed by scientific prestige. Some of those claims later circulated outside physics as if they were established lessons of quantum mechanics. The later misuse cannot be understood without the earlier authority that made the language attractive.

The question "At whom are we laughing?" therefore has more than one answer.

We may be laughing at cultural theorists who invoke physics they do not understand. We may also be laughing, without noticing it, at ideas first promoted by major physicists. And we may be watching a disciplinary hierarchy determine in advance which obscurities count as profundity and which count as fraud.

## Why this belongs in the Hegel project

The useful lesson here is methodological.

A project trying to formalize philosophical claims should be strict about distinctions between:

- the mathematics;
- an interpretation of the mathematics;
- an analogy exported from one domain to another;
- the authority of the person making the analogy;
- and the independent philosophical argument, if any, that survives after the scientific prestige is removed.

That standard should be applied symmetrically. Hegel does not become rigorous because a mathematician likes him, and a philosophical claim does not become foolish merely because a physicist laughs at it.

Beller's article is valuable because it demands the same skepticism toward authorized scientific philosophy that Sokal demanded toward postmodern uses of science.
