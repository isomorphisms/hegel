# Critical Text

*The journal in the Sokal affair was **Social Text**. This note is titled **Critical Text** as requested.*

The standard retelling of the Sokal hoax is simple: physicist Alan Sokal wrote a deliberately nonsensical article, filled it with fashionable political and theoretical language, sent it to *Social Text*, and the editors published it because it flattered their ideological commitments.

The editors' own response, Bruce Robbins and Andrew Ross's "Mystery Science Theater" in *Lingua Franca* (July/August 1996), makes the episode both worse and more interesting than that one-line version.

## What the editors actually said

Robbins and Ross did **not** claim that they had read Sokal's manuscript and found it a brilliant piece of physics or philosophy.

They said almost the opposite. The manuscript struck them as odd. They described it as "hokey" and unlike the other pieces around it. They had asked Sokal to remove a substantial amount of philosophical speculation and much of the footnote apparatus. According to their account, he resisted revision, and at one point they regarded him as a difficult author whose manuscript might simply be too much trouble to publish.

Later, when they assembled the special "Science Wars" issue, the article acquired a context in which they thought it might be worth printing. They interpreted it as an unusual intervention by a professional physicist trying to engage postmodern philosophy and science studies. They expected readers to encounter it as an offbeat contribution from someone outside the field, not as an authoritative statement of the journal's own position.

They also emphasized that *Social Text* at the time was not a conventional refereed scientific journal. It was a journal of political opinion and cultural analysis. They therefore did not send Sokal's physics to physicists for technical peer review.

After the hoax was disclosed, they said plainly that they regretted publishing the article.

## Their defense

The editors made several distinct arguments, some stronger than others.

First, an editorial failure at *Social Text* does not by itself discredit the whole enterprise of studying science historically, politically or sociologically. Even a spectacularly embarrassing acceptance decision cannot establish that every claim made in science studies is false.

Second, they rejected the media caricature that the journal had been exposed as literally disbelieving in the existence of the physical world. Their work questioned scientific authority, institutions, agendas, funding priorities, public accountability and the social organization of knowledge. That is not the same proposition as "the external world does not exist."

Third, they objected to the method of the hoax. Sokal represented himself as a politically sympathetic contributor acting in good faith, while privately constructing a trap. Robbins and Ross treated this deception as a breach of scholarly ethics and, because Sokal identified himself with the political left, as a particularly destructive form of left-on-left warfare.

Those are genuine points. A successful hoax has to be interpreted carefully: what exactly did the experiment test, and what conclusions actually follow from it?

## Where their response is weakest

The editors' own explanation also identifies the central editorial failure more clearly than many hostile summaries do.

They already had doubts about the manuscript. They thought it was strange, overstuffed and philosophically excessive. They did not understand or verify enough of its technical content to determine whether the physics supported the conclusions. Yet the fact that the author was a professional physicist, apparently sympathetic to their politics and apparently eager to connect his field with theirs, counted in favor of publication.

That is close to Sokal's proposed failure mode.

The relevant problem is not simply that the editors were ignorant of physics. Editors cannot be experts in everything. The problem is that **authority and ideological congruence substituted for checking the part they did not understand**.

Their description of Sokal as an "earnest" scientist seeking some affirmation from postmodern philosophy is especially revealing. It suggests that the manuscript was interesting partly because of what its author's disciplinary identity and political posture appeared to signify. The contribution was not being judged solely by whether its arguments survived scrutiny.

The ethical complaint about deception does not erase this. Sokal may have acted deceptively and the journal may also have failed editorially. Both can be true.

## What the hoax demonstrates

The strongest conclusion is narrower than either camp often wanted.

The hoax demonstrates that this editorial process could be vulnerable to a manuscript that:

- came from an author carrying scientific authority;
- displayed the expected theoretical vocabulary;
- appeared politically friendly;
- made claims the editors were not equipped to verify;
- and was weak enough that the editors themselves had already noticed serious problems.

That is a real and important failure.

What the hoax does **not** demonstrate by itself is that cultural studies is meaningless, that social analysis of science is invalid, that every theorist Sokal cited is fraudulent, or that scientific institutions are somehow free of analogous prestige and conformity effects.

Those larger propositions require their own arguments and evidence.

## Read together with Mara Beller

Mara Beller's later essay, "The Sokal Hoax: At Whom Are We Laughing?", makes the episode harder to use as a simple morality play.

Beller shows that famous physicists including Bohr, Born, Heisenberg and Pauli themselves made sweeping extrapolations from quantum mechanics into politics, psychology, religion and culture. Some of the rhetoric later mocked as postmodern excess had been authorized earlier by extremely prestigious scientists.

The two texts therefore expose different parts of the same problem.

Sokal shows that ideological friendliness and disciplinary authority can weaken editorial scrutiny.

Robbins and Ross show, often unintentionally, how that failure actually occurred and why a journal might have treated a strange contribution generously.

Beller shows that the boundary between "scientific rigor" and "philosophical nonsense" does not coincide neatly with the boundary between physicists and humanists. Scientific prestige can protect bad philosophy just as theoretical prestige can protect bad uses of science.

The useful conclusion is not that everybody is equally right. It is that **arguments have to be separated from the prestige, politics and disciplinary identity of the person making them**.

## Why this belongs in the Hegel project

This repository is attempting something that could easily reproduce either side of the Sokal failure.

It would be a mistake to use category theory, dependent types or proof assistants as scientific decoration that makes a vague Hegelian claim look rigorous. It would also be a mistake to assume that a philosophical proposal is meaningless merely because it does not already look like mathematics.

The protection against both errors is the same:

1. state the philosophical claim separately;
2. state the mathematical structure separately;
3. state the proposed interpretation connecting them;
4. verify the mathematics;
5. and do not pretend that verifying the mathematics automatically proves the interpretation.

That is a stricter lesson than "Sokal won" or "Sokal was mean." It is also more useful.
