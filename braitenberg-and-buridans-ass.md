# Braitenberg, Buridan's ass, and practical free will

Buridan's ass is a very small problem disguised as a very large one. An agent has two exactly balanced alternatives, equally desirable and equally accessible. If choice requires a sufficient reason favoring one alternative, what makes the agent choose at all rather than remain stuck forever?

Valentino Braitenberg's *Vehicles: Experiments in Synthetic Psychology* is useful here because it reverses the ordinary direction of psychological explanation. Instead of observing a creature and inferring a hidden mind, Braitenberg builds extremely simple mechanisms and asks what an outside observer would say about their behavior. A few sensors, motors, crossed connections, thresholds, memories, and feedback loops rapidly produce behavior that observers describe with words such as fear, aggression, love, preference, deliberation, foresight, and free will.

## A Buridan problem can be mechanically cheap

The important example comes early. In Vehicle 4, threshold devices can delay a response until activation becomes strong enough. To an observer, the delay looks like hesitation followed by decision. Braitenberg remarks that one is tempted to say that where decisions are being made there must be a will making them.

But the mechanism may be almost embarrassingly simple. Nothing inside the vehicle has to contain a miniature philosopher comparing reasons. A threshold, a tiny asymmetry, accumulated activation, noise, memory of a previous state, or interaction with the environment is enough to break a tie.

That is already a useful answer to Buridan's ass. Exact motivational symmetry does not require a mysterious faculty of pure choice. Real dynamical systems have histories, thresholds, perturbations and internal state. Two alternatives can be descriptively equal at one level while the physical system is nowhere near exactly symmetrical at every relevant level.

This does **not** solve the general problem of free will. It mainly dissolves one supposed paradox about how action can begin when explicit reasons are tied.

## Uphill analysis and downhill invention

Braitenberg calls this the law of "uphill analysis and downhill invention." It is easy to construct a simple mechanism that produces a behavior; it is much harder to infer the mechanism from the behavior alone. We routinely overestimate internal complexity when looking from the outside.

This matters for psychology. A person who pauses, changes direction, approaches, withdraws, learns a preference, or appears conflicted may be implementing a complicated reflective process. But the behavior alone does not prove that. Many different internal mechanisms can generate the same observable trajectory.

The inverse is also important: a relatively simple mechanism can generate behavior whose consequences are difficult even for its designer to foresee. Simple causal structure does not imply simple observable behavior.

## Braitenberg's stronger free-will claim

By Vehicle 12, Braitenberg has built a sufficiently complicated network of threshold devices and learned associations that its future internal activity is practically unpredictable. He provocatively calls this evidence for free will: even knowing the mechanism, an observer may be unable to predict what the vehicle will do next, and the vehicle itself cannot completely predict its own next state.

This is best read as an **operational** account of freedom, not a proof that causation has disappeared. Unpredictability and metaphysical indeterminism are different claims. A system can be causally determined while remaining impossible to forecast in practice because the state is inaccessible, the computation is too large, perturbations amplify, or prediction itself interacts with the system.

Braitenberg's more interesting point is therefore not that he refutes determinism. It is that the social and psychological role played by "free will" may require much less than an uncaused cause. If neither other people nor the agent can cheaply predict the agent's behavior, then domination by simple prediction becomes difficult and the agent experiences its own future as genuinely open.

## The practical questions remain

Buridan's ass asks: **how can a choice occur when reasons are tied?**

The questions that matter in ordinary life are larger:

- Can another person alter what I want rather than merely predict what I will choose?
- Can advertising, sales technique, institutional pressure or repeated exposure reshape the salience and value of alternatives?
- Can I learn to make better decisions, or merely become a more complicated causal machine?
- When is a person's behavior sufficiently under their control that praise, blame, punishment or responsibility make sense?
- How should responsibility change under coercion, ignorance, addiction, manipulation, fatigue, developmental limitation, or badly designed institutions?

Braitenberg does not answer these questions. But his method changes how they should be asked. If preferences, hesitation, commitment and apparent spontaneity can all emerge from mechanisms, then the relevant contrast is not simply **caused versus free**. We need to ask what kinds of causal organization constitute deliberation, self-correction, responsiveness to reasons, resistance to manipulation, and control over one's own future behavior.

A salesperson who makes me want something has not disproved my freedom merely by affecting me; no human desire develops without causes. The harder question is whether I can recognize, evaluate and revise the causes acting through me. Likewise, responsibility need not require total control. Nobody has total control. It may instead depend on degrees and kinds of control: knowledge, foresight, capacity to respond to reasons, freedom from coercion, and the ability to alter one's conduct.

## Why this belongs in the Hegel project

Braitenberg is useful here precisely because he makes a crude metaphysical opposition look less useful. A mechanically caused action can still be internally differentiated, historically formed, self-modifying and difficult to predict. That pushes the question away from a mythical choice with no antecedents and toward the organization of a system's relation to its own determinants.

That is closer to the practical problem of freedom: not *whether anything caused me*, but *what kind of relation I have to the causes, habits, desires, institutions and other people through which I act*.

Buridan's ass is therefore a boundary case, not a theory of freedom. Braitenberg gives a convincing synthetic explanation of how the boundary case can disappear. The interesting work starts after the ass moves.
