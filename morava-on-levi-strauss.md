# Morava on Lévi-Strauss

Jack Morava’s short paper *On the canonical formula of C. Lévi-Strauss* is a useful model for how a mathematician can approach a difficult piece of work from another discipline without either pretending that the mathematics is obviously sound or dismissing it as nonsense.

Morava begins from a position of real mathematical skepticism. Lévi-Strauss’s canonical formula for myth is under-specified by ordinary mathematical standards: the domain is unclear, quantifiers are missing, and the symbols change roles in a way that looks strange if one reads the expression as an ordinary equation. Morava does not hide any of this. But he also refuses to infer from unfamiliar notation that Lévi-Strauss simply does not know what he is doing. His working assumption is that there is something coherent there to be translated.

That distinction matters. The problem may not be that one discipline has failed to speak the language of another correctly. It may be that the two disciplines have different conventions for what can remain implicit, what counts as an object, and what a formal expression is supposed to do. Morava explicitly describes the difficulty as one of translation between anthropology and mathematics, disciplines that normally do not spend much time talking to one another.

His proposed translation is concrete. He reads the canonical formula not primarily as an equality but as describing a transformation between two structured analogies. He then exhibits a mathematical model for that transformation using an anti-automorphism of the quaternion group of order eight. The point is not that quaternion groups somehow explain mythology. The point is narrower: a precise mathematical structure exists in which the formal transformation Lévi-Strauss appears to be describing can actually occur.

This is exactly the kind of distinction that is easy to lose when mathematics is imported into philosophy or the human sciences. There are at least three separate questions:

1. Can the proposed structure be made mathematically coherent?
2. Is that formalization uniquely determined by the original text?
3. Does the resulting model tell us anything true or useful about its empirical subject matter?

Morava’s 2003 note addresses mainly the first question. His later sequel is even more explicit that he is not claiming uniqueness and is not primarily settling the empirical truth of Lévi-Strauss’s theory. He is asking whether the formula can be interpreted as a genuine formal mathematical assertion, and constructing one candidate model.

There is also an important social fact around the paper. Morava was married to Ellen Contini-Morava, a linguistic anthropologist, so anthropology was not simply an alien discipline observed from across a departmental border. It would be too strong to claim that this fact caused the method of the paper: Morava himself specifically credits the anthropologist Fred Damon for discussions that helped him understand the literature. But the broader intellectual situation is worth noticing. The paper reads like the work of someone accustomed to the possibility that another discipline has its own internal standards and that understanding those standards may require conversation rather than ridicule.

That is especially striking because the easy response to Lévi-Strauss’s mathematical notation is ridicule. Morava instead does something harder. He identifies exactly what bothers a mathematician, keeps those objections intact, and then asks whether a charitable reconstruction can turn the apparent defect into a precise structure. In the 2003 paper he concludes, in effect, that Lévi-Strauss knows what he means to say.

For this repository, that is a useful methodological precedent. Formalizing Hegel, Lawvere, psychoanalysis, anthropology, or any other nonstandard source should not mean granting every mathematical metaphor legitimacy in advance. It should mean reconstructing the strongest precise version we can find, distinguishing the reconstruction from the source text, and then checking the mathematics without pretending that mathematical consistency settles the interpretation.

The right standard is neither reverence nor debunking. It is translation followed by verification.
